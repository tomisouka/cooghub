01
Permutations & Combinations
P(n,k) · C(n,k) · n^k · ASYMPTOTIC RANKING · 30 PTS
▶
Problem
There are \(n\) students and \(k \leq n\) movie tickets. In how many different ways can \(k\) tickets be distributed among the \(n\) students if:
a
Tickets for different movies. Each student gets at most one. (8 pts)
b
Tickets for different movies. Each student can get any number \(\leq k\). (7 pts)
c
Tickets for the same movie. Each student gets at most one. (7 pts)
d
Rank (a), (b), (c) by asymptotic growth w.r.t. \(n\), assuming \(k = \lceil n/2 \rceil\). Use Big-O or Big-\(\Theta\). (8 pts)
Part A — Different Movies, At Most One Each
Formula
P(n,k) = n!/(n−k)!Ordered · No Repetition
▶
\[ P(n,k) = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} \]
Order matters because tickets are distinguishable (different movies). No repetition because each student gets at most one.
Prerequisite Knowledge▶
Two questions determine your formula: (1) Does order matter? (2) Is repetition allowed?

Here tickets are for different movies, so getting ticket A ≠ getting ticket B. Order matters → permutation. Each student gets at most one → no repetition.
\[ \underbrace{n}_{\text{1st ticket}} \times \underbrace{(n-1)}_{\text{2nd ticket}} \times \cdots \times \underbrace{(n-k+1)}_{k\text{-th}} = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} \]
1
Identify: ordered, no repetition
Distinct tickets → order matters. At most one per student → no repetition. This is a permutation.
2
Apply multiplication principle
\[ n \times (n-1) \times \cdots \times (n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} \]
✓ Solution\[ P(n,k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} \]
Part B — Different Movies, Any Number Per Student
Formula
n^kOrdered · Repetition Allowed
▶
\[ n^k \]
Each of the \(k\) distinct tickets independently goes to any of \(n\) students — repetition is allowed.
Prerequisite Knowledge▶
Key difference from Part A: a student can now receive multiple tickets. After giving ticket 1, all \(n\) students are still eligible for ticket 2.
\[ \underbrace{n \times n \times \cdots \times n}_{k\ \text{times}} = n^k \]
1
Identify: ordered, repetition allowed
Distinct tickets → order matters. No restriction on count → repetition allowed.
2
Apply multiplication principle with repetition
\[ n \times n \times \cdots \times n = n^k \]
✓ Solution\[ n^k \]
Part C — Same Movie, At Most One Each
Formula
C(n,k) = n!/k!(n−k)!Unordered · No Repetition
▶
\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \]
Tickets are identical → only which \(k\) students get one matters, not the order.
Prerequisite Knowledge▶
Why unordered? All tickets are for the same movie — indistinguishable. Start from \(P(n,k)\) and divide out the \(k!\) orderings of the chosen students that are identical.
\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \]
1
Identify: unordered, no repetition
Same ticket for all → order doesn't matter. At most one per student → no repetition. This is a combination.
2
Divide permutations by over-count
\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \]
✓ Solution\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \]
Part D — Asymptotic Ranking with \(k = \lceil n/2 \rceil\)
Key Facts
Stirling: n! ≈ (n/e)^nC(n,n/2) = Θ(2^n/√n)
▶
\[ n! \approx \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n \sqrt{2\pi n} \qquad \binom{n}{n/2} = \Theta\!\left(\frac{2^n}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \]
Use Stirling's approximation to simplify factorials at \(k = n/2\).
1
Substitute \(k = n/2\) into each answer
\[ \text{(a)}\ \frac{n!}{(n/2)!} \qquad \text{(b)}\ n^{n/2} \qquad \text{(c)}\ \frac{n!}{(n/2)!\,(n/2)!} \]
2
Analyze (c) — slowest
\[ \binom{n}{n/2} = \Theta\!\left(\frac{2^n}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \]
By Stirling: the \((n/e)^n\) terms cancel leaving \(2^n\). Choosing a subset is far fewer arrangements than assigning distinct tickets.
3
Analyze (a) and (b) — fastest
\[ P(n,n/2) = n(n-1)\cdots(n/2+1) = \Theta(n^{n/2}) \qquad n^{n/2} = \Theta(n^{n/2}) \]
(a) and (b) are the same asymptotic order. Both grow as \(\Theta(n^{n/2})\), which dominates \(2^n\) since \(\sqrt{n} \gg 2\) for large \(n\).
✓ Ranking — slowest to fastest \[ \underbrace{(c)}_{\Theta(2^n/\sqrt{n})} \;\ll\; \underbrace{(a) \approx (b)}_{\Theta(n^{n/2})} \]
Intuition: (c) counts subsets — just who gets a ticket. (a) counts ordered selections — who gets which movie. (b) allows repeats. At \(k=n/2\), (a) and (b) both reach \(\Theta(n^{n/2})\), dwarfing the exponential \(2^n\) of (c).
Concept
[CH1] COUNTING[CH1] ASYMPTOTIC NOTATION
▶

Two questions determine your formula. Does order matter? Is repetition allowed? Order + no rep → \(P(n,k)\). Order + rep → \(n^k\). No order + no rep → \(\binom{n}{k}\).

Asymptotic hierarchy: \(\log n \ll n \ll n^c \ll c^n \ll n! \ll n^n\). Binomial \(\binom{n}{n/2} = \Theta(2^n/\sqrt{n})\) is exponential but still much slower than \(n^{n/2}\) which grows as \((\sqrt{n})^n\).

02
Mathematical Induction
WEAK INDUCTION · DIVISIBILITY · 30 PTS
▶
Problem
Prove by mathematical induction: for any positive integer \(n\), \(6^n - 1\) is divisible by \(5\).
1
What is the induction variable? (2 pts)
2
What is the base case? (3 pts)
3
What is the induction hypothesis? (7 pts)
4
State what to prove in the induction step and prove it. (15 pts)
5
Did you use weak or strong induction, and why? (3 pts)
Part 1 — Induction Variable
1
Identify the variable
The statement is about all positive integers \(n\). We induct on \(n\) — stepping through \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\)
✓ AnswerThe induction variable is \(n\).
Part 2 — Base Case
What is a base case?▶
The smallest value of \(n\) — here \(n=1\). You must verify the claim directly by computation. No induction allowed here.
1
Set \(n=1\), evaluate
\[ 6^1 - 1 = 5 \]
2
Verify divisibility
\[ 5 \mid 5 \quad \checkmark \]
✓ Base Case: n = 1\[ 6^1 - 1 = 5, \quad 5 \mid 5 \quad \checkmark \]
Part 3 — Induction Hypothesis
What is an induction hypothesis?▶
The assumption we take for free — we assume the statement holds for \(n\) and use it to prove it for \(n+1\). It is NOT something we prove in this step.
1
State the hypothesis formally
\[ \textbf{IH:}\ \text{Assume } 5 \mid (6^n - 1),\ \text{i.e., } 6^n - 1 = 5m\ \text{for some integer } m. \]
✓ IH\[ 5 \mid (6^n - 1) \iff 6^n - 1 = 5m \text{ for some } m \in \mathbb{Z} \]
Part 4 — Induction Step
Goal
Prove 5 | (6^{n+1} − 1)Using IH
▶
Rewrite \(6^{n+1} - 1\) so the IH term \((6^n - 1)\) appears explicitly — then substitute.
Key trick: \(6^{n+1} - 1 = 6 \cdot 6^n - 1 = 6(6^n - 1) + 5\)
1
Rewrite to expose \((6^n - 1)\)
\[ 6^{n+1} - 1 = 6 \cdot 6^n - 1 = 6 \cdot 6^n - 6 + 5 = 6(6^n - 1) + 5 \]
2
Substitute IH: \(6^n - 1 = 5m\)
\[ 6^{n+1} - 1 = 6(5m) + 5 = 30m + 5 = 5(6m + 1) \]
3
Conclude
\[ 5(6m+1),\ 6m+1 \in \mathbb{Z} \implies 5 \mid (6^{n+1} - 1) \quad \checkmark \]
✓ Induction Step \[ 6^{n+1} - 1 = 6(6^n-1)+5 = 6(5m)+5 = 5(6m+1) \implies 5 \mid (6^{n+1}-1) \quad \blacksquare \]
Part 5 — Weak or Strong?
1
Check what the step required
To prove \(P(n+1)\) we only used \(P(n)\) — the immediately prior case. We did not need \(P(n-1), P(n-2), \ldots\)
2
Classify
\[\textbf{Weak:}\ P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1)\] \[\textbf{Strong:}\ P(1) \wedge \cdots \wedge P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1)\]
One step back → weak induction.
✓ AnswerWeak induction — proving \(P(n+1)\) required only \(P(n)\).
Concept
[CH2] INDUCTION[APP-A] WEAK vs STRONG
▶

Three parts — never skip any. Base case: verify directly. IH: assume for \(n\). Step: prove for \(n+1\) using IH only.

The algebraic trick for divisibility: rewrite \(f(n+1)\) so \(f(n)\) appears as a factor. The IH handles that factor; the remainder is explicit.

Weak vs Strong: if the step looks back one case → weak. If it might need any earlier case (e.g. Fibonacci, coin problems) → strong.

03
Recursive Algorithm Design
DIVIDE & CONQUER · INDUCTION PROOF · RECURRENCE · 40 PTS
▶
Problem
Array \(A\) of 0s and 1s is good if it has an even number of 1s. Example: \([1,0,0,1]\) is good; \([1,1,1,0,0,1,1]\) is not.
a
Give a recursive algorithm (pseudocode) to determine if \(A[1\ldots n]\) is good. (20 pts)
b
Prove correctness using mathematical induction. (10 pts)
c
Give a recurrence for the runtime. (5 pts)
d
Solve the recurrence and state the runtime. (5 pts)
Part A — Recursive Algorithm
Strategy
Divide & ConquerSplit · Recurse · XOR
▶
\[ \text{parity}(A) = \text{parity}(\text{left half}) \oplus \text{parity}(\text{right half}) \]
XOR: same parity ⊕ same parity = 0 (even total). Different parities ⊕ = 1 (odd total).
Prerequisite — Divide & Conquer▶
D&C template: Split → Solve recursively → Combine.

Parity observation: total 1s = (1s in left) + (1s in right). The sum is even iff both halves have the same parity. XOR captures this: \(0 \oplus 0 = 0\), \(1 \oplus 1 = 0\), \(0 \oplus 1 = 1\), \(1 \oplus 0 = 1\).
1
Base cases
Empty array: 0 ones → even → return 0. Single element: return A[lo] directly (it is 0 or 1, which is already the parity).
2
Pseudocode
IsGood(A, lo, hi) Output: 0 = even ones (good) · 1 = odd
1if lo > hi: return 0  empty → 0 ones
2if lo = hi: return A[lo]  single element
3mid ← ⌊(lo + hi) / 2⌋  split point
4pL ← IsGood(A, lo, mid)  left parity
5pR ← IsGood(A, mid+1, hi)  right parity
6return pL ⊕ pR  XOR = overall parity
✓ Call: IsGood(A, 1, n) = 0 → goodReturns 0 if \(A\) has even # of 1s, else 1.
Part B — Correctness by Induction
Induction on array size n▶
Claim: IsGood(A, lo, hi) returns the parity of the number of 1s in A[lo..hi]. Induction variable: \(n = \text{hi} - \text{lo} + 1\).
1
Base cases: \(n=0\) and \(n=1\)
\(n=0\): returns 0. Empty → 0 ones → parity 0. \(\checkmark\)
\(n=1\): returns \(A[\text{lo}]\). Value is 0 or 1 = its own parity. \(\checkmark\)
2
IH: correct for all sizes \(< n\)
Both halves have size \(\leq \lfloor n/2 \rfloor < n\), so by IH, \(p_L\) and \(p_R\) are correct parities.
3
Induction step: \(n > 1\)
\[ (a + b) \bmod 2 = (a \bmod 2) \oplus (b \bmod 2) \implies \text{parity(total)} = p_L \oplus p_R \quad \checkmark \]
✓ Correctness provedBy IH both halves return correct parities; XOR gives correct overall parity. \(\blacksquare\)
Part C — Recurrence
1
Count calls and work per level
2 recursive calls on arrays of size \(n/2\). Combine step: one XOR + one split = \(O(1)\).
✓ Recurrence\[ T(n) = 2T\!\left(\tfrac{n}{2}\right) + O(1), \quad T(1) = O(1) \]
Part D — Solve the Recurrence
Master Theorem
a=2, b=2, f(n)=O(1)Case 1
▶
\[ T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n) \xRightarrow{\text{Case 1}} T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a}) \text{ when } f(n) = O(n^{\log_b a - \varepsilon}) \]
Case 1 applies when the recursive work dominates the combining work.
1
Identify \(a, b, f(n)\)
\[ a = 2,\quad b = 2,\quad f(n) = O(1) \implies \log_b a = \log_2 2 = 1 \]
2
Check Case 1: is \(f(n) = O(n^{1-\varepsilon})\)?
\[ O(1) = O(n^0) = O(n^{1-1}) \implies \varepsilon = 1 > 0 \quad \checkmark \]
3
Apply Case 1
\[ T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 2}) = \Theta(n) \]
✓ Runtime\[ T(n) = \Theta(n) \]
Sanity check: any algorithm that counts 1s must inspect every element at least once → \(\Omega(n)\) lower bound. Our algorithm hits this exactly — it's optimal.
Concept
[CH3] RECURSION[CH3] RECURRENCES[CH2] INDUCTION
▶

D&C = Split · Solve · Combine. Correctness proof mirrors the algorithm: base case = termination condition; induction step = correctness of combine given correct subresults.

Recurrences encode recursive runtime. \(T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(1)\): 2 subproblems of half size, constant combine. Master Theorem Case 1 → \(\Theta(n)\).

Common Master Theorem results: \(T(n)=T(n/2)+O(1) \Rightarrow O(\log n)\). \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+O(1) \Rightarrow O(n)\). \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+O(n) \Rightarrow O(n\log n)\).
